# Compliance deadlines, not dashboards, open the door

Sports organizations in late 2026 are not mainly asking for more analytics. They want to cut the manual, error-prone work of moving information between departments that were never built to talk to each other. The sharpest version of that need is in US college athletics. There, the House settlement turned compliance, cap tracking and athlete payments into a two-ledger accounting problem, and a single January portal window squeezes it into about two weeks. The best independent data on tooling shows that **76% of football practitioners still analyze data in spreadsheets and 73% store it in more than one system**. Peer-reviewed research also finds that silos come as much from culture, power and medical confidentiality as from software. Pro teams are adopting horizontal AI assistants quickly: 91% of the Jets front office uses Copilot daily. But their own executives say this has not yet produced "large business gains." Agentic coordination layers exist only as pilots, such as the Sabres' deal with SprintAI. Buying is dominated by Teamworks, which claims 99% of D-I departments and wins follow-on contracts as sole-source buys on integration grounds. Every major incumbent shipped an AI assistant in 2025-26, and each one works only on its own product's data. So a vendor-neutral, cross-department coordination agent is an open position, but a contested one. It is most defensible where a deadline or a regulator forces information across departments: CSC/NIL Go submissions, eligibility certification, and daily availability reporting. Much of the evidence on pain points comes from vendors, and almost nothing independent quantifies hours lost, so the thesis rests on strong qualitative and moderate quantitative support.

## How strong the evidence is, and where it comes from

The research base is uneven, and that matters for a founder deciding what to believe. The strongest evidence comes from a few independent sources: the Knight Commission/Elon survey of 376 Division I leaders, two peer-reviewed studies of football and collegiate practitioners, NCAA infractions releases, public procurement records, CSC statistics reported by CBS Sports and AP, and Catapult's audited results. Moderately strong evidence comes from on-record quotes from team executives in ESPN, theScore, Sky Sports and SBJ (relayed by PFT). Weak evidence is mostly vendor or partner content: Teamworks blogs and case studies, Kitman, Hudl and SprintAI launch posts, OpenAI's Spurs write-up, and Microsoft's NFL materials. This weak category is where most of the vivid "spreadsheets and email chains" language comes from. Much of the 2026 MIT Sloan material is session-description copy written by organizers. It shows what topics were programmed, not what was said. The table below gives the overall calibration.

| Claim | Evidence strength | Main basis |
|---|---|---|
| Spreadsheets and fragmented storage dominate data work | Strong (independent survey, n=206) | Dello Iacono et al. 2025 |
| Silos are cultural and political as well as technical | Strong qualitative (n=28 focus groups) | King et al. 2024 |
| NIL Go is backlogged and was not designed for associated deals | Strong (CSC's own data and CEO quotes) | CBS Sports, AP, FOS |
| Revenue-share admin runs on spreadsheets, email and manual payments | Weak to moderate (vendor list, consistent with independent anecdotes) | Teamworks blog |
| Handoff failures between academics and compliance carry real penalties | Strong (official NCAA findings) | NCAA releases |
| Pro teams want automated reporting and coordination | Moderate (a few named deployments, mostly early) | Sabres, Jets, NHL reporting |
| Hours lost to manual reporting | No data found | — |

## Revenue sharing created a second ledger and a two-week crunch

The post-House pain shows up first as budget anxiety and then as operational overload. In the only rigorous post-settlement survey, fielded in the first weeks of revenue sharing, **79% of D-I leaders were concerned about relying on institutional funds and student fees to balance budgets**, and 48% were "extremely concerned" ([Elon Poll](https://www.elon.edu/u/elon-poll/knight-commission-on-intercollegiate-athletics-division-i-leaders-survey/finances-and-operations-of-college-athletics/)). Respondents also said **86% believe the transfer portal hurts Division I**, and 55% expect female athletes to be worse off ([Knight Commission](https://www.knightcommission.org/2025/10/survey-results-of-division-i-leaders-released/)). The survey measured expectations, not lived workflows, and its respondents lean toward faculty athletics representatives and non-FBS schools. The 94 ADs are the subgroup closest to operations, and the margin of error for that subgroup is larger than the overall ±4.4%.

The operational detail comes from the enforcement system. NIL Go was designed to clear **up to 90% of deals automatically**. Instead, "associated" deals from collectives, multimedia rights holders and apparel companies made up 63% of Jan-Feb 2026 deals and 78% of their value, and they need investigator review. That volume spiked in step with the January portal window ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/college-sports-commissions-nil-clearinghouse-strained/)). Front Office Sports reported the same 78% as a share of submissions, against a design assumption of about 10%. The sources disagree on whether 78% describes value or volume. CSC CEO Bryan Seeley said the software must change and there is "no quick fix" ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/college-sports-commission-says-nil-go-system-under-strain/)). His summary: "A lot of this is problems with the system just not being designed to handle this" ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/college-sports-commissions-nil-clearinghouse-strained/)). Reported review times are about **50% within 24 hours and 70% within seven days**, but only *after all required information is submitted*. As of January 1, 2026, 524 deals worth $14.94M had been rejected ([ESPN/AP](https://africa.espn.com/espn/story/_/id/47591684/college-watchdog-group-rejected-500-plus-nil-deals-worth-nearly-15-million)). That qualifier hides the back-and-forth over missing documents, which is where compliance staff time goes. Seeley himself says some schools and businesses resist providing information. The CSC's January 2026 guidance requires third-party deals of $600 or more to be reported within five days. It also warns against "making promises … now and figuring out how to honor those promises later" ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/name-image-and-likeness-nil-compliance-alert-for-higher-education-deal-rejections-mount-as-state-resistance-grows)). Meanwhile, the stalled participant agreement, state attorney general resistance (Texas, Tennessee) and Nebraska players' arbitration challenge mean compliance offices must reconcile CSC rules with conflicting state laws. Federal preemption is not coming soon: the SCORE Act's floor vote was canceled again in May 2026 ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/article/as-score-act-vote-gets-canceled-a-new-college-sports-bill-may-emerge/)). The CSC data in these notes runs only through March 2026.

On the revenue-share side, Teamworks says departments run the process across "spreadsheets for budgeting, email chains for contracts, manual payment processing, and separate systems for tax reporting." It adds that "decisions slow down and money gets misallocated" when teams use different data ([Teamworks](https://teamworks.com/blog/revenue-sharing-teamworks)). This is a vendor selling the fix, and it gives no figures. But it agrees with independent reporting. Minnesota's NIL business development manager said of stakeholders: "there's no question that they don't know how this all works" ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/nil-has-birthed-a-third-party-cottage-industry-and-its-a-mess/)). Some collectives "have stopped reporting deals through the system" ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/the-year-schools-paid-their-players/)). Most FBS schools put **70-75% of revenue share into football**, which creates the Title IX exposure the surveys flag ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/the-year-schools-paid-their-players/)).

The calendar makes all of this worse. FBS collapsed to **one portal window, January 2-16**, and cut the coaching-change window from 30 to 15 days. Transfers now arrive mid-semester, which burdens academic staff, and playoff teams get almost no time ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/article/football-transfer-portal-chaos-continues-despite-new-rules/)). Inside those two weeks, evaluation, offers, revenue-share contracting, NIL Go submission under the five-day rule, and academic enrollment all overlap. That is the clearest pinch point across GM, compliance, NIL, academics and business-office staff in any of the evidence. Collective heads describe being on the phone "almost every minute of the day," and one had close to 100 unread texts by 11 a.m. ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/article/football-transfer-portal-chaos-continues-despite-new-rules/)). Schools are adding expensive GMs, some paid $500K to $1.5M ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/college-football/story/_/id/44789751/college-football-gm-new-gold-rush)), while cutting support staff elsewhere: Indiana eliminated 25 positions and Kansas cut 8-10% of its workforce ([Athletic Business](https://www.athleticbusiness.com/operations/budgeting/article/15712608/indiana-preps-for-revenue-sharing-by-eliminating-25-athletics-positions); [KU Sports](https://www2.kusports.com/sports/college/general/2025/may/20/ku-athletics-has-cut-workforce-by-8-to-10-expects-to-save-3-million/)). The result is more coordination work spread across fewer people. One gap stands out: no named compliance officer or NIL director appears on record describing their workload in hours.

## Spreadsheets run three-quarters of football data work, and people ration information

The most useful independent number in the research comes from Dello Iacono et al.'s 2025 survey of 206 practitioners in professional and semi-professional football across all six FIFA confederations, 68% of them in UEFA. **76% use spreadsheets to aggregate and analyze data, 62% use them for reporting, and 73% store data in more than one service** ([Dello Iacono et al. 2025](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/638479/9/Data%20analytics%20practices%20and%20reporting%20strategies%20in%20senior%20football%20%20insights%20into%20athlete%20health%20and%20performance%20from%20over%20200%20practitioners%20worldwi.pdf)). Reporting cadence is high. Top-tier clubs report to performance staff daily (67%), to coaches daily (63%) and to medical staff daily (50%). The authors explain spreadsheet dominance by cost, setup time and perceived ease of use, along with "financial constraints, and/or expertise-related issues." Ten years of athlete-management-system marketing has not displaced the spreadsheet. AMS platforms sit alongside it.

In US college sport, one Division I university's staff identified **28 distinct technologies, with individual teams using between 6 and 23**. Interns and tech coordinators capture and report data, and the work falls to coaches when those roles don't exist. One team lost its longitudinal data after a department switched tools ([Brewer et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1644099/full)). Cross-department flow depended on one person, a Director of Sports Performance and Analytics who acted as an "interdisciplinary translator." An athletic trainer said, "I trust him immensely with the information that he's given me." This is a small, single-site sample, but the pattern is telling: coordination is done by a human hub. That is the role an AI "staff member" would try to fill.

The founder-relevant caution is that silos are not only technical. King et al.'s 2024 focus groups with 28 high-performance practitioners found that information is treated as a tradeable commodity. People decide individually what to share and tend to under-share. Medical confidentiality is a recurring barrier ([King et al. 2024](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/54261/1/54261%20King%20et%20al.%20VOR.pdf)). Their quotes are blunt: "a culture where there's a sense of stuff's not shared, and it almost immediately breeds division"; "people saying this is my domain, how dare you step into it"; and "I'd be guilty probably of under sharing." On the medical-performance interface, one participant said: "It's hard to navigate… we need some of that information so that we can help performance." A 2020 survey of 434 collegiate athletic trainers documents coach pressure to return athletes early, and finds that DI trainers perceive more coach influence over their jobs ([Journal of Athletic Training](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/55/3/article-p303.xml)). These findings set a limit on any coordination tool. An agent that pipes medical data to coaches would run straight into confidentiality and power dynamics. An agent that enforces agreed rules about what gets shared, with whom and when, could reduce them.

Vendor case studies supply concrete versions of the same picture. Stoke City's Head of Medical said, "We were using four separate systems to manage player data," and described compiling the daily availability schedule for the manager as "extremely difficult" ([Teamworks/Stoke City](https://teamworks.com/blog/stoke-city-fc-collecting-and-centralizing-data-to-improve-reporting/)). A Teamworks staffer says he has asked hundreds of practitioners how they check travel schedules, and the answers ranged up to "WhatsApp and a spreadsheet" ([Teamworks](https://teamworks.com/blog/pre-season-tech-stack-review-for-performance-and-operations-leaders/)). Independent reporting from Texas adds a vivid case: baseball coach Jim Schlossnagle still scans spreadsheets with a highlighter, and Texas football "knows its data isn't being fully used" ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/article/kirk-goldsberry-leads-texass-ai-push-and-understands-the-skeptics/)).

The clearest documented *cost* of coordination failure is in eligibility certification, where academics, compliance, the registrar and coaches all meet. At Manhattan College, confusion over whether certification belonged to "athletics academic advisors or athletics compliance officers" led to **26 athletes across six sports competing in 521 contests while ineligible**. The result was probation, vacated records and a roughly $120,000 investment in compliance ([NCAA](https://www.ncaa.org/news/media-center-manhattan-college-failed-to-monitor-eligibility-certification/)). At Findlay, a single overwhelmed compliance director's missed checklists left 82 athletes improperly certified ([NCAA](https://www.ncaa.org/news/2023/9/13/media-center-findlay-failed-to-monitor-eligibility-certification.aspx)). Both cases predate House, but the failure mode is exactly what more paperwork and leaner staffs make more likely. No comparable quantified case was found in European football or in scouting-to-coaching handoffs.

## Sloan's agenda shifted from "use analytics" to "organize analytics"

MIT Sloan 2026's programming shows that front-office concerns have moved from whether to use data to how to integrate and operationalize it. Sessions included "From Tool Sprawl to Competitive Advantage: Rebuilding Coherent Analytics," "Beyond the Eye Test: How AI is Capturing What Coaches Know," and "The Analytics Paradox" ([SSAC 2026](https://sloansportsconference.com/conference/2026-conference)). MLSE's Devin Pleuler spoke on centralizing multi-sport R&D, where "true specialists are impractical," and on "how AI might reverse the trend toward hyper-specialization" ([SSAC](https://sloansportsconference.com/event/building-unfair-advantages-analytics-at-multi-sport-organizations)). The college track moved from settlement governance in 2025 to a 2026 panel arguing that schools are leaving "isolated decision-making" behind for "enterprise-wide business intelligence" and "shared data systems" ([SSAC](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/event/reimagining-the-ncaa-data-as-the-new-competitive-advantage)). One caveat: the tool-sprawl session was vendor-presented (Lumenalta), and no press recaps were found for it, for the AI sessions, or for the Hinkie-led "Is AI the Answer?" panel. The agenda signals the theme. It does not report what practitioners said.

What speakers did say on record frames the friction as trust and translation, not missing models. The 76ers' Ariana Andonian said: "We think the data is giving us an answer, when actually it's giving us some information." Seattle Storm coach Sonia Raman said: "It's not like you can just get an AI report in the middle of the game that says, 'Get some shooting in'" ([MIT News 2026](https://news.mit.edu/2026/mit-sloan-sports-analytics-conference-shows-why-data-makes-difference-0310)). In 2025, Shane Battier noted that analysts rarely talk directly to players, and Sarah Rudd said video is how soccer analysts "get on the same page" with coaches ([MIT News 2025](https://news.mit.edu/2025/five-ways-to-succeed-sports-analytics-0313)). On AI, the 2025 consensus was "a vote, not the decider." Daryl Morey said, "We absolutely use models as a vote in any decision... We'll treat them almost as one scout" ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2025/06/24/nba-draft/nba-draft-artificial-intelligence)). Jonathan Kraft said AI "will never fully replace a coach or a scout," but will make their jobs more efficient ([Boardroom](https://boardroom.tv/2025-mit-sloan-conference-key-takeaways/)). No reported Sloan remark addressed AI agents or workflow automation directly. Other practitioner conferences (StatsBomb, Leaders, SBJ Intercollegiate, NACDA, vendor user events) were not covered by this research. That is a real gap, because NACDA and compliance conferences are where college buyers actually shop. College of Charleston's procurement file says its compliance-software market research was done at a national compliance conference.

## Pro teams adopt assistants widely but agents only in pilots

Pro-team AI use in front offices splits in two. Horizontal assistants are spreading quickly, mostly through league-level Microsoft and OpenAI relationships. Vertical, agentic workflow tools are at the pilot stage. The Jets went from "a handful" of Copilot users to **91% of the front office using it daily** in about 100 days, averaging 2-3 prompts a day. Their Chief Data & Analytics Officer, Iwao Fusillo, oversees both business and football analytics. He calls this "level one... adoption" and admits, "Do we have large business gains from that level one? Not really. But have we changed the culture?" ([PFT/SBJ](https://www.nbcsports.com/nfl/profootballtalk/rumor-mill/news/jets-new-front-office-culture-is-to-think-ai-first)). GM Darren Mougey says the main benefit so far is "the automation of things," saving "a lot of man hours" ([NewYorkJets.com](https://www.newyorkjets.com/news/darren-mougey-jets-training-camp-utilizing-ai-analytics-07-29-2026), club PR). The Spurs rolled ChatGPT out across their business organization, with custom GPTs for summarizing decks, drafting reviews and cleaning data ([OpenAI Forum](https://forum.openai.com/public/blogs/inside-the-spurs-ai-playbook-from-fan-engagement-to-community-impact-2025-11-21), vendor source). At the league level, the NFL put Copilot+ PCs on every sideline and gave analysts a Copilot Excel dashboard. It lists "operations dashboards and Copilot-generated game summaries" as still in development ([Insider Sport](https://insidersport.com/2025/08/26/nfl-microsoft-copilot-sideline-technology-2025/)).

The deployment closest to the startup idea is the **Buffalo Sabres' September 2026 partnership with SprintAI**. SprintAI is building an "intelligence and orchestration layer" for hockey operations that links existing data sources, flags items needing staff attention, automates routine reporting and keeps institutional knowledge. A Performance Staff module is live, and a GM module is being co-developed. GM Jarmo Kekäläinen described the goal as helping "our people leverage AI to connect and interpret the information we already have" ([NHL.com/Sabres](https://www.nhl.com/sabres/news/buffalo-sabres-sprint-ai-partnership)). This validates the concept: a pro team bought exactly this framing. It also tells you a competitor exists, and no outcome data has been published yet. NHL analytics directors describe using generative AI, Claude included, to automate everything from data collection to presentation. One executive warned, "There's this competitive advantage window that's opened because of AI... And I think most teams are unprepared" ([theScore](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/3533715/how-ai-is-impacting-the-way-nhl-teams-do-business)).

Executives consistently put guardrails on decision-making, not on workflow. Vikings interim GM Rob Brzezinski said "you have to train the AI to interpret the data the way you want... It's still in its infancy stages." Rams GM Les Snead calls AI an "assistant lieutenant" ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/48446759/nfl-draft-combine-artificial-intelligence-caleb-downs-arvell-reese-david-bailey)). The one hard league rule so far is MLB's mid-2026 ban on generative AI for in-game decisions on dugout iPads, after reports that up to a third of teams were using tablets that way ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2217032/mlb-bans-using-dugout-ipads-for-ai-powered-in-game-strategy-calls)). Back-office, between-games coordination is currently unregulated. European football is earlier still. Reading's Head of AI runs a recruitment proof of concept, and match analysis is "some way off." Reading appointed a former manager specifically to bring coaches on board ([Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/12040/13483738/readings-head-of-ai-explains-ambition-to-be-pioneers-as-league-one-club-looks-for-an-edge-to-reach-the-premier-league)). Manchester City hired a Head of AI, and Arsenal is hiring engineers to "automate analysis workflows" ([TGG](https://trainingground.guru/manchester-city-appoint-power-as-head-of-ai/); [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/artificial-intelligence/story/can-ai-help-win-football-matches-arsenal-is-hiring-an-engineer-to-plan-wins-heres-how-to-apply-545185-2026-07-24)). Below the elite tier, teams cannot build their own. Most MLS clubs have 1-3 full-time analytics staff, five have none known, and very few employ data engineers ([American Soccer Analysis](https://www.americansocceranalysis.com/home/2025/7/20/state-of-mls-analytics-july-2025)). Elite clubs build, mid-tier clubs need packaged agents, and the mid-tier is where a vendor product fits.

## Teamworks controls the procurement path, and mandated work gets funded first

Technology spending is structurally hard to see in college athletics. The NCAA financial reporting categories used by Knight-Newhouse have no software line. Tech spend is spread across "Other Expenses," staff and equipment, while FY2025 added a dedicated "Institutional NIL Revenue Share" line ([Knight-Newhouse](https://knightnewhousedata.org/about-the-data)). No benchmark for tech spend as a share of budget exists in the notes. Consultants (Deloitte 2026, PwC's 9th Global Sports Survey) call AI strategic but publish no budget figures. Deloitte expects adoption to start in back-office workflows such as finance reconciliation and ticket renewals, after data consolidation and governance are in place, and predicts more AI agents for end-to-end workflows ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-telecom-outlooks/sports-industry-outlook.html)).

Public procurement records show who buys and why. At College of Charleston, the **Athletics Compliance Department** requested Teamworks Compliance and Recruiting (**$91,550 over four years**). The justification was the risk of "NCAA violations and institutional fines," and the record notes that market research at a national compliance conference found only Teamworks had robust rules engines ([CofC](https://charleston.edu/ebid/sole-source/25-44-sdm-ss-t5-ncaa-compliance.pdf)). A year later, Teamworks Hub (**$446,800 over four years**) was approved as sole source by the Director of Procurement and the EVP for Business Affairs. The reason given was that it is the only platform that integrates with modules the school already owns, and that separate systems would mean duplicate entry and multiple logins ([CofC](https://charleston.edu/ebid/sole-source/26.11.jcb.ss.t4-teamworks-hub.pdf)). That puts a mid-major's full-platform spend at roughly $135K a year. CBS Sports puts Teamworks' basic football package at mid-five figures, rising to six figures with add-ons, and reports that personnel departments now weigh AI spending directly against athlete pay ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/meet-the-new-recruit-artificial-intelligence-in-college-football)). On the pro side, Catapult's average ACV per pro team passed **$30K a year** for the first time, with retention above 96% ([Motley Fool AU](https://www.fool.com.au/2026/05/20/catapult-sports-reports-record-revenue-in-fy26/)). That is a useful benchmark for a single category.

The buyer pattern is consistent. A functional lead (compliance, operations, or a sport) champions the purchase, a deputy AD or CFO funds it, and university procurement and IT approve it. A new entrant must pass a campus HECVAT security review, under which universities tier vendors by risk and apply FERPA, HIPAA and GDPR terms ([UpGuard](https://www.upguard.com/blog/tprm-for-collegiate-athletics); [EDUCAUSE](https://www.educause.edu/higher-education-community-vendor-assessment-toolkit/hecvat-faqs-for-higher-education)). It must also clear state bidding thresholds or win a sole-source case. Incumbents win sole-source cases on integration, and challengers rarely can. There are two documented workarounds. Ten of 12 MAC schools adopted Teamworks GM through the conference office with a group discount and reportedly bypassed traditional procurement ([Teamworks/MAC](https://teamworks.com/blog/mac-implements-teamworks-general-manager/)). And affiliated entities such as Boise State's foundation-housed BroncoPRO now handle cap management and payments ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/schools-are-scrambling-to-prepare-for-the-ncaa-revenue-sharing-era)), which may sit outside state procurement rules. Teamworks' scale makes it the gatekeeper. It raised $235M at a valuation above $1B in June 2025 and more at over $1.5B in April 2026. It claims 99% of D-I departments and 100% of NFL teams, and it has acquired INFLCR, ARMS, Smartabase, Zelus, Telemetry, Sportlogiq and PFF's enterprise business ([Teamworks Series F](https://teamworks.com/blog/teamworks-series-f/); [Teamworks/Hg](https://teamworks.com/blog/hg-growth-investment/)). These penetration figures are vendor claims. The Premier League figure moved from 90% to 100% between the two posts. Sales-cycle length and how pro teams buy remain undocumented in this research.

## Demand for a cross-department agent is real, but each incumbent stays inside its own data

The case *for* demand rests on four independent lines of evidence. First, the problem is documented: spreadsheet dominance, fragmented storage, human "translator" hubs, and costly handoff failures. Second, buyers are acting. The Sabres bought an orchestration and reporting layer. The Jets and NHL analysts are automating reporting work. Texas launched Bevo Lab with Dell, Google Cloud and SHI to build natural-language tools across 21 programs ([SVG](https://www.sportsvideo.org/2026/10/26/texas-athletics-and-mccombs-business-of-sports-institute-launch-bevo-lab-for-sports-ai-and-analytics/)). USC and Arizona have hired AI leads. Nebraska uses AI agents to narrow about 14,000 portal names to four targets ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/meet-the-new-recruit-artificial-intelligence-in-college-football)). Third, staff already use ChatGPT on their own to condense scouting reports into bullet points for coaches ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/meet-the-new-recruit-artificial-intelligence-in-college-football)). That is a reporting-automation need being met with unsanctioned tools. Fourth, AI-workspace startups are getting traction. Dropback went from a two-person YC company to "30+ elite teams" and a Hudl IQ integration ([Hudl](https://www.hudl.com/blog/hudl-dropback-partner)). Kitman says customization was "the most-requested capability" in its platform's history ([iSportConnect](https://www.isportconnect.com/kitman-labs-and-google-cloud-redefine-sports-analytics-with-my-ip-launch/)).

The case *against* is just as concrete. Every incumbent has shipped its own assistant, scoped to its own data. Ask Teamworks handles policy and protocol inside Teamworks products. Hudl's Signal AI Assistant works only on Signal data ([Hudl](https://www.hudl.com/blog/hudl-signal-ai-assistant)). Kitman's My iP produces scheduled cross-team reports across performance, medical, coaching, talent and operations, but only on data in Kitman. Teamworks also runs AI-readiness sessions that include "a simple framework for evaluating any AI vendor before you sign" ([Teamworks](https://explore.teamworks.com/ai)), which positions it as the gatekeeper for third-party AI. Staff ask whether tools are "software for software's sake" and whether a problem "needs AI or just a database." Oregon's Dan Lanning says it is "still the early days" ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/meet-the-new-recruit-artificial-intelligence-in-college-football)). Vendors have failed: SANIL shut down, Blueprint fell from 25+ clients to 10, and Opendorse cut 23% of staff ([FOS](https://frontofficesports.com/nil-has-birthed-a-third-party-cottage-industry-and-its-a-mess/); [Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/nil-tech-platform-opendorse-reduced-its-workforce/)). Those failures were NIL intermediaries, not ops software, but they show buyers churn fast. Athletes push back on monitoring features ([QU Media](https://qumedia.net/2025/02/25/quincy-university-athletes-share-concerns-over-teamworks-nutrition-app/)). The Jets' CDAO admits broad AI adoption has not produced large gains. Elite programs build in-house, and Microsoft's league-level deals make Copilot the default. Nobody in the evidence sells exactly a vendor-neutral, cross-department AI staff member. The closest players are SprintAI (pro hockey), Sherpa Sports (an Azure-based "all-in-one" ops platform with no disclosed customers, per [GSIC](https://www.sport-gsic.com/blog/new-members-3/sherpa-sports-joins-gsic-powered-by-microsoft-to-transform-sports-intelligence-and-operations-85)), Kitman My iP and Teamworks AI. The gap exists, but Teamworks is the natural fast follower and owns much of the data an agent would need.

## Where to aim first

The evidence points to a clear order of attack. **First, target the compliance and revenue-share reconciliation loop in college athletics.** This means pre-validating NIL Go submissions, chasing the missing documents that stall the seven-day clock, reconciling cap-counted revenue share against CSC-reviewed third-party NIL, and producing the audit trail. This work is mandated, deadline-driven and cross-departmental. Compliance offices buy on violation risk, which is the justification that actually got Charleston's purchase through procurement. Schools that lack Power-4 headcount feel it most. **Second, target eligibility and academic-compliance handoffs**, especially for mid-semester transfer arrivals after the January window. Here the cost of failure is documented in NCAA penalties, and ownership between academics and compliance is demonstrably blurred. **Third, target the daily availability report and recurring cross-team packs** (medical to coaching to GM, recruitment meeting packs, AD and board summaries). These are high-frequency, spreadsheet-built and named in vendor case studies, and they are the use case the Sabres bought. The medical-data permissions problem means the product must enforce sharing rules, not bypass them.

Avoid decision-making features (player valuation, in-game advice). That space is crowded (Dropback, Teamworks/PFF, Hudl IQ), increasingly regulated (MLB's ban), and executives explicitly reserve it for humans. Build to integrate with Teamworks, Hudl and Kitman, not to replace them, and pursue partnerships like Hudl-Dropback. Sell through conferences or affiliated entities to get around campus procurement. Get HECVAT and SOC 2 ready early. Prove value in hours saved and violations avoided, because every dollar competes with athlete pay. The biggest unknowns to test in customer discovery are how many hours compliance and ops staff actually spend on these reports, whether Teamworks will grant API access to the data a neutral agent needs, and whether mid-tier pro clubs, with 1-3 analysts and no data engineers, will pay for a SprintAI-style layer.

## Conclusion

The market is not asking for an AI that thinks better than its staff. It is asking for something that carries information across department lines that people, politics and confidentiality rules keep closed. That reframes the product. The winning "AI staff member" is less an analyst and more a compliance-aware chief of staff: it knows which deadline is coming, which department holds the missing document, and what may be shared with whom. The post-House regime has turned that role from a nice-to-have into a source of legal exposure.

The structural risk is that incumbents are converging on the same idea from inside their own data. A startup's lasting advantage would have to be neutrality across vendors and ownership of the workflows that cross them, and Teamworks is better placed than anyone to close that gap through acquisition or API policy. The near-term opening is real because the CSC system is still being rebuilt, the January crunch repeats every year, and mid-tier schools and clubs cannot build their own tools. That window is measured in seasons, not years.
